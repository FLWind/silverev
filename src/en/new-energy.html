2011–2019

Silver, new energy technologies and the foundations of the EV era

As silver prices fell after the 2011 peak, the metal gained new roles in silicon anodes and solid-state cells. Solar power became a major consumer, while the lithium-ion industry built the foundations for more ambitious battery projects in the 2020s.

Introduction: markets and technology diverge

At first glance, 2011–2019 looks disappointing for silver. In late April 2011, its price approached $50 per troy ounce before a long decline. Investment interest weakened, predictions of an immediate shortage did not materialise and the mass battery market committed to lithium-ion.

The technological story was different. Silver began taking on more targeted roles: conductive coatings on silicon anodes, nanoparticles inside porous structures, solid-state electrode components and a model ionic carrier for studying solid electrolyte–electrode interfaces.

Three broader changes occurred alongside this work. Solar power became a major industrial demand source. The US shale revolution changed expectations of future oil scarcity. Production EVs, charging networks and large-scale lithium-ion manufacturing gradually created the industrial base that would support next-generation solid-state and lithium-metal projects.

Silver-coated silicon anodes: a prelude to the decade

An important paper appeared in May 2010, just before this period and silver's 2011 rise towards $50. Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Solid State Research and Tohoku University created three-dimensional macroporous silicon coated with silver nanoparticles.

Silicon's much higher theoretical capacity made it a prospective replacement for graphite, but it undergoes major volume changes during lithiation. Particles crack, electrical contacts break and capacity declines. The porous structure provided room for expansion, while silver improved electronic conductivity and reversible lithium storage.

This was not a conventional silver anode. Silver served as a thin functional material around high-capacity silicon. A small quantity of expensive metal at a critical location would become a recurring principle in advanced battery research.

2011: silver moves inside porous silicon

In October 2011, researchers at Hong Kong Polytechnic University and Wuhan University presented three-dimensional porous silicon microparticles containing silver nanoparticles. Unlike the preceding surface coating, the conductive particles were embedded within an interconnected silicon matrix.

The structure addressed two tasks. Pores partly accommodated silicon's expansion, while silver maintained electronic pathways as geometry changed. After 50 cycles, the electrode retained about 784 mAh/g of discharge capacity, more than twice graphite's theoretical capacity of approximately 372 mAh/g.

Fifty cycles cannot establish commercial suitability, particularly for an EV. Nevertheless, the study marked a shift: silver was being considered as a way to improve lithium-ion chemistry rather than compete with lithium.

2012: a fully solid-state silver battery

In January 2012, a French group published All-solid-state silver batteries assembled by Spark Plasma Sintering. It demonstrated a bulk all-solid-state cell:

Ag0.7V2O5 / Ag6I4WO4 / Ag0.7V2O5.

The cell was assembled in one step using Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS), a process combining pulsed electric current and pressure to densify and join the layers rapidly.

Early bulk solid-state cells suffered from poor contact between solid particles as well as electrolyte limitations. Cold-pressed electrodes thicker than 400 μm showed strong polarisation because of inadequate electrode–electrolyte interfaces. SPS produced dense, thick layers with stable interfaces. Cells showed good reversibility without continuous external pressure during cycling.

The authors explicitly noted the limited standalone commercial potential of the silver chemistry. Its benefit was as a model: high room-temperature silver-ion conductivity made solid-state interface mechanics and electrochemistry easier to study. The results were intended to inform bulk solid-state lithium-ion development.

Why this study matters: The 2012 silver cell was not a direct predecessor of a modern EV battery. It was an experimental platform for a central solid-state challenge: forming a stable interface between a thick electrode and a solid electrolyte.

Samsung in 2011–2013: early patent groundwork

During the same period, Samsung SDI and Samsung's Japanese research organisation increased patent activity in solid electrolytes, solid-state electrodes and large-cell assembly.

A Samsung SDI patent with March 2011 priority described a solid electrolyte for rechargeable lithium batteries. Permitted conductive additives included metal powders and fibres such as copper, nickel, aluminium and silver. Another patent with June 2011 priority covered solid electrolytes and lithium batteries, including EV applications, and mentioned silver among possible alloy and electrode constituents.

In December 2011, Samsung Yokohama Research Institute filed for a solid-state electrode with a separate conductive bonding layer between electrode and current collector. In December 2012, Samsung R&D Institute Japan filed several applications addressing sulfide electrolytes, electrode geometry and damage prevention in laminated solid-state cells.

These documents show Samsung treating solid-state batteries as a coordinated field by the early 2010s: electrolyte, electrode, interface, current collector, binders and increasing cell area.

Was a silver technology already concealed in early patents? That possibility cannot be ruled out, but the available documents do not establish it. Silver usually appears among many possible conductive metals, dopants or alloy constituents. There is no explicit description of a silver–carbon layer equivalent to the Ag–C composite in Samsung's 2020 paper.

Broad patent language can protect many alternatives and limit disclosure of a specific development route. The claim that Samsung deliberately concealed Ag–C because of excitement in the silver market remains a hypothesis. The more cautious conclusion is that the company was building a patent foundation in which silver remained an available engineering material.

ZPower: conventional silver–zinc reaches a market

Ag–Zn development continued alongside lithium and solid-state research. Zinc Matrix Power, later ZPower, had worked on silver–zinc since the 1990s, drawing in part on published NASA and military-laboratory research.

The company addressed silver and zinc electrodes, electrolyte, separators and charging algorithms. It sought to suppress dissolution and shape change, silver migration and zinc dendrites. Commercial development increased reported life to 500–1,000 cycles without significant capacity loss.

In late 2013, ZPower launched a rechargeable silver–zinc hearing-aid battery. The small format was important: the smaller the cell, the less silver cost affects the entire device. Ag–Zn combined high specific energy, non-flammable aqueous chemistry and all-day operation in a compact case.

Silver–zinc had survived lithium-ion's rise by finding a niche where safety, size and energy density mattered more than minimum raw-material cost.

The 2011 peak and quantitative easing

The conventional market explanation of silver's rally was largely monetary and investment-driven. In November 2010, the Federal Reserve announced QE2, purchasing $600 billion of longer-term Treasury securities through June 2011. Near-zero interest rates continued amid investor concerns about inflation, dollar weakness and the global financial crisis.

The Silver Institute identified investment as the main driver in 2010: global silver investment rose 40% to 279.3 million ounces. In 2011, the annual average price reached a record $35.12 per ounce, with total global silver investment estimated at about $10 billion.

The timing makes a QE connection plausible, but insufficient as a complete explanation. In September 2012, the Fed began QE3 with $40 billion in monthly mortgage-backed-security purchases, later adding $45 billion of longer-term Treasuries per month. Despite the longer programme, silver did not regain its 2011 peak. Its 2013 average price fell to $23.79 even as physical demand reached a record.

Expansion of the Fed's balance sheet did not determine the price automatically.

Solar panels: a real but adaptable consumer

Unlike laboratory batteries, photovoltaics consumed tens of millions of ounces annually. Silver paste formed conductive contacts on silicon solar cells. PV demand reached approximately 75.8 million ounces in 2011, then a record.

Rapid industry growth led to forecasts of more than 100 million ounces by 2015. That did not happen. High silver prices and competition accelerated thrifting, reducing metal per cell. Manufacturers narrowed conductive lines, improved pastes and printing, and explored replacing silver with copper or other materials.

PV demand fell in 2012 before recovering to 77.6 million ounces in 2015, 76.6 million in 2016 and approximately 92 million in 2017. In 2018 it fell 9% under pressure from thrifting and substitution, then rose 7% in 2019 to the second-highest level recorded at that time.

The solar-demand explanation is therefore only partly sufficient. PV became a large, persistent consumer, but was not passive: higher silver costs encouraged lower loading per watt. Growth in panel installations and reduced metal per panel worked in opposite directions.

The shale revolution: coincidence or a changing energy narrative?

The shale revolution did not begin suddenly in 2014; that year exposed its accumulated scale. Preparation started much earlier. The US Department of Energy funded unconventional-resource research in the 1970s–1990s, and advances in horizontal drilling, multistage hydraulic fracturing and geological modelling gradually made tight-rock extraction commercially viable.

An important policy and investment shift came in the mid-2000s. The 2005 Energy Policy Act created a public–private unconventional oil and gas research programme worth about $400 million over eight years. High oil prices and available capital also attracted private companies to the Barnett, Bakken, Eagle Ford and other formations.

Government supported research and infrastructure, producers acquired leases and improved drilling, and investors began anticipating substantial growth in US output. By 2010, shale supplied around 23% of US dry natural gas production. Oil output, which had largely declined since the mid-1980s, increased in both 2009 and 2010.

Thus, by silver's spring 2011 peak, shale had not reached its eventual production scale, but substantial commitment to its development already existed. Markets could reassess the long-term outlook before additional millions of barrels appeared in official statistics.

This mattered to the prevailing energy narrative. Much of the 2000s commodity market anticipated oil scarcity, depletion and rising extraction costs, implying a faster transition to alternative energy and electric transport. Successful horizontal drilling and fracturing suggested another scenario: previously inaccessible reserves could reach markets relatively quickly, shifting the United States from growing import dependence towards greater abundance.

Shale does not directly explain silver's reversal after April 2011. The initial fall began too early and was more closely associated with overheated futures positions, higher margin requirements, the end of QE2 and changing investment expectations. However, success in the Bakken, Eagle Ford and Permian during 2011–2013 may have weakened the commodity-supercycle argument of inevitable energy scarcity.

By 2014, the accumulated effect was clear. US oil output increased by roughly 1.2 million barrels per day, the largest absolute annual increase in the country's historical statistics. Brent fell from approximately $112 per barrel in June to $62 in December.

Shale was not the sole cause of the oil-price collapse. Growing US supply coincided with weak demand growth, recovering production elsewhere, major OPEC producers' decision not to cut output and a stronger dollar. Yet shale became a symbol of the shift from expected hydrocarbon scarcity to relative abundance.

Cheaper petrol and a delayed EV transition

Lower oil prices may also have temporarily weakened the economic incentive to switch from combustion cars to EVs. Cheaper petrol reduces potential fuel savings, making the high initial price of early EVs more significant.

This effect was particularly relevant in the United States, where relatively low fuel taxes make retail prices more sensitive to oil markets. The IEA identified cheap petrol as a possible contributor to a 4% decline in US electric-car sales in 2015. Sales continued growing in China and some European markets with subsidies, environmental rules and industrial policies, while the US market paused.

It would be wrong to claim that shale stopped the EV transition. Lithium-ion costs continued falling, Tesla expanded, charging networks grew and automakers developed new platforms. Cheap oil could nevertheless have delayed adoption by reducing its urgency while EVs remained expensive and reliant on policy support.

For silver, the possible connections were mainly indirect:

  • greater oil supply reduced current inflation and inflation expectations;
  • the investment narrative of inevitable energy scarcity and a continuing commodity supercycle weakened;
  • a stronger dollar put pressure on silver and other dollar-denominated commodities;
  • cheaper petrol temporarily reduced EVs' economic appeal;
  • slower EV adoption deferred a large market for new battery technologies that might use silver in anodes, interfaces and solid electrolytes.

Early EVs with conventional lithium-ion batteries did not themselves create substantial direct battery demand for silver. The possible effect worked mainly through expectations about how quickly new energy technologies would replace oil infrastructure and when experimental silver-containing solutions might reach a mass market.

Working hypothesis: Shale was not the immediate cause of silver's spring 2011 collapse. However, unconventional oil and gas development had been supported and financed well before peak output. In 2010–2013, expected US production growth may already have changed perceptions of energy scarcity. When output and lower oil prices confirmed those expectations in 2014, cheaper fuel weakened inflation and commodity narratives and may have temporarily slowed EV adoption. Coincidence with silver's prolonged decline does not establish causation, but merits examination alongside QE, dollar and investment-demand explanations.

EV infrastructure grew while silver prices fell

The decade's main battery story was industrial scale-up of existing lithium-ion technology. The Nissan LEAF and Chevrolet Volt launched in late 2010, the Tesla Model S in 2012 and BMW i3 in 2013, while Model 3 deliveries scaled in the second half of the decade. Major cell, power-electronics and battery-management suppliers joined established automakers and startups.

The IEA records roughly 17,000 plug-in electric cars on the world's roads in 2010. By the end of 2019, the stock reached 7.2 million and annual sales exceeded 2.1 million. Market share remained small, but manufacturing was no longer experimental.

Average automotive lithium-ion pack prices fell from more than $1,100 per kWh in 2010 to about $156 in 2019. Chemistry improvements, greater energy density, factory automation, volume growth and standardisation of modules and cooling systems contributed.

Charging infrastructure expanded too. US public and private charging ports increased from about 5,070 in 2011 to more than 85,000 in 2019. Fast-charging networks, shared protocols, diagnostics, government programmes and large traction-battery factories emerged.

This infrastructure mattered beyond conventional lithium-ion. By the decade's end, the industry could:

  • manufacture battery materials and cells at scale;
  • assemble packs storing tens of kilowatt-hours;
  • manage thousands of cells through battery management systems;
  • provide cooling, safety systems and fast charging;
  • test batteries against automotive standards;
  • build charging networks and service infrastructure.

The lithium-metal anodes, solid electrolytes, silicon, Ag–C composites and interfacial coatings of the 2020s therefore arrived in an established ecosystem. They could draw on EV demand, factories, equipment suppliers, automotive testing programmes and a clear economic objective.

Conclusions: groundwork rather than an obvious revolution

The years 2011–2019 did not see silver return to batteries at mass scale. Prices declined after 2011, lithium-ion captured the market and laboratory silver use was too small to affect global demand materially.

But several directions emerged that would later become important:

  • silver as a conductive coating on high-capacity silicon anodes;
  • silver nanoparticles within porous composites;
  • silver-ion solid-state systems as models for interface research;
  • major manufacturers' patent portfolios for electrodes and solid electrolytes;
  • commercial development of rechargeable Ag–Zn for specialised devices;
  • large but continually optimised solar-sector demand;
  • scale-up of lithium-ion production and EV infrastructure.

Monetary explanations account for much of the 2010–2011 rally, but subsequent QE showed that monetary expansion alone was insufficient. Solar power became a major silver consumer while rapidly reducing loading. Shale did not cause the initial price reversal, but later changed energy and inflation expectations.

The decade's lesson is not that markets accurately priced a future silver battery in advance. Financial enthusiasm cooled while laboratories and patent departments were identifying new functions for the metal. By 2020, two foundations were in place: scientific work on more complex battery architectures and the industrial ecosystem of mass-market EVs.

A prelude to the next period: In 2020, Samsung presented a silver–carbon composite for a solid-state lithium-metal battery. Although it appeared new, the 2010s show at least a decade of research into silver in conductive layers, silicon composites, solid interfaces and electrodes.
