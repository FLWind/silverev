The first silver–zinc batteries

How high specific energy turned an expensive laboratory system into a power source for some of the most demanding tasks of the mid-twentieth century.

From an idea to a practical system

Electrochemists knew the silver–zinc combination long before the twentieth century, but early cells were laboratory demonstrations rather than convenient rechargeable batteries. Both electrodes deteriorated, active materials migrated through the cell and performance declined markedly after repeated charging. By the early 1930s, silver–zinc chemistry was attractive in theory but still lacked a sufficiently reliable design for widespread use.

The turning point is associated with the French researcher Henri André. In the late 1920s and throughout the 1930s, he sought a way to physically separate the electrodes without obstructing ion transport through the alkaline electrolyte. This did not eliminate every weakness, but it made a compact battery that could be recharged as well as discharged a practical possibility.

Why silver and zinc attracted engineers

The principal advantage was high energy output for relatively little mass and volume. A silver-oxide positive electrode and a zinc negative electrode operated in aqueous potassium hydroxide. The battery could deliver substantial current while maintaining a fairly flat discharge voltage, valuable properties when a power source had to be both compact and powerful.

Those benefits came at a literal price: silver was expensive and early rechargeable cells had limited life. The technology was not initially intended to replace inexpensive lead–acid batteries in cars, industry or stationary installations. Its natural niche was applications where saving a kilogram or a litre mattered more than the cost of the battery.

A defining feature of the period: Silver–zinc developed as a specialised technology for applications requiring high power, compactness and reliable activation, rather than a mass-market energy source.

Military programmes in the 1940s

The Second World War sharply increased demand for lightweight, powerful independent energy sources. US military agencies, particularly the Navy and aviation organisations, continued developing silver–zinc systems, with substantial attention to primary and reserve batteries. In reserve designs, electrolyte could be stored separately until activation, allowing a battery to remain dormant for a long period before rapidly delivering high power.

Military programmes helped turn individual laboratory solutions into reproducible technology. Development also continued on rechargeable designs. The main obstacles were changes in the shape of the zinc electrode, conductive growths and progressive damage to the separating material.

Henri André and the separator problem

André's key contribution was a thin, permeable membrane between the positive and negative electrodes. It had to allow electrolyte and ion transport while retaining particles of active material. Migration of those materials had previously accelerated cell deterioration and internal short circuits.

André's 1946 patent application described a silver–zinc rechargeable battery with insulating separators intended to increase cycle life. The documents specifically addressed gradual enlargement of separator pores. This illustrates the period's engineering challenge: the chemical couple was already known, and progress depended largely on the materials and design of internal components.

From invention to production

In the second half of the 1940s, André's work became associated with Yardney International, founded by engineer Michel Yardney. The company pursued patents, improvements and commercialisation. By the early 1950s, designs were being developed for serial assembly, mechanical strength and minimum size, as well as laboratory performance.

A Yardney patent filed in 1949 and published in 1952 explicitly aimed at a compact, lightweight rechargeable battery suitable for mass production. Electrodes containing silver, zinc and their oxides were held under pressure in a case with little free electrolyte. The central question had shifted from whether the system could work to how it could become a practical product.

The 1950s: aviation, marine applications and backup power

During the 1950s, silver–zinc batteries established a place in aviation, marine and specialised military equipment. Post-war aviation needed lighter DC backup power. Reports from the mid-1960s noted that lead–acid batteries had dominated this role roughly a decade earlier, while emerging alkaline systems offered better performance per unit mass and volume.

Electrodes, housings and intercell connections improved in parallel. Mid-1950s patents described thinner silver electrodes, silver-foil contact layers and compact packs with fewer connecting wires. Individually modest changes collectively made the system suitable for equipment where power failure could cause the loss of costly hardware or endanger lives.

By the end of the decade, silver–zinc batteries were regarded as mature specialised power sources rather than experimental curiosities. They remained too expensive for consumer and automotive markets, but their high specific energy and power justified their cost in torpedoes, underwater equipment, aviation and other demanding applications.

The price of high power

Even after the improvements of the 1950s, two basic limitations remained: the cost of silver and the comparatively short active life of rechargeable versions. Zinc dissolved and redeposited unevenly, changed shape and could form structures that damaged the separator. The silver electrode and separating material also progressively degraded.

The technology consequently developed along two tracks. Primary and reserve batteries were valued for storage life and immediate high power after activation. Rechargeable versions served applications where weight savings justified limited cycle life and more demanding maintenance. This distinction persisted in later decades.

The period's legacy

From the 1930s to the end of the 1950s, the essential characteristics of silver–zinc technology took shape. André's work showed that the thin membrane between electrodes could be as important as electrode chemistry. Military programmes accelerated compact reserve batteries, and Yardney developed the scientific idea into an industrial design.

By the start of the 1960s, the technology was ready for the rocket and space age. Its use in early spacecraft and crewed programmes built on that established foundation. The central lesson was that expensive silver could be justified by a distinctive combination of power, compactness and reliability, even without a mass market.
